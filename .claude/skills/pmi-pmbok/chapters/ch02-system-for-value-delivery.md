# Chapter 2: A System for Value Delivery

## Core Idea
Value delivery is produced by an integrated system — portfolios, programs, projects, products, and operations acting together inside an internal environment (itself inside a larger external environment) — and it depends on continuous information/feedback flow between senior leadership, portfolios/programs/projects, and operations/products.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **System for Value Delivery (Figure 2-2)**: Portfolio Management + Strategic Planning sit at the center, linking Organizational Strategy/Vision/Mission to Management of Authorized Programs and Projects and Management of Operations and Products, all inside the Internal Environment, inside the External Environment.
  - When to use: diagnosing why a "successful" project didn't produce organizational value — check whether the feedback loop (Figure 2-3) back to senior leadership is actually closed.
  - How: trace desired outcomes/benefits/value from strategy → down to portfolios/programs/projects → and verify performance information flows back up.
- **Two-dimension project success assessment**: (1) success of project **outcomes** (value realized, possibly long after delivery) vs. (2) success of project management **processes** (cost/scope/time/quality adherence). Both matter; they can diverge sharply.
  - When to use: before declaring a project a "success" or "failure" — specify which dimension you're judging.
  - How: ask separately "did this hit its constraints baseline?" and "did this ultimately create the intended value?"
- **EEFs vs. OPAs**: Enterprise Environmental Factors (conditions the project team doesn't control, internal or external to the org) vs. Organizational Process Assets (the org's own policies/processes/procedures + knowledge repositories).
  - When to use: classifying any planning input — "is this a constraint we didn't create" (EEF) or "is this an internal asset we can tailor" (OPA)?

## Key Concepts
- **Business value**: net quantifiable benefit, tangible (monetary assets, productivity, market share) or intangible (goodwill, brand, reputation) — see Figure 2-1.
- **Enterprise Environmental Factors (EEF)**: internal (culture, infrastructure, IT systems, resource/employee capability, financial capability) or external (marketplace, legal, academic research, government/industry standards, emerging tech, public health regulations).
- **Organizational Process Assets (OPA)**: policies/processes/procedures (usually PMO-controlled, tailorable per org policy) + organizational knowledge repositories (updated continuously: financial data, lessons learned, issue/defect data, metrics, project files).
- **Organizational structure types**: organic/simple, functional, multidivisional, matrix (weak/balanced/strong), project-oriented, virtual/network, hybrid — each with different PM authority and resource availability (Table 2-1).
- **Product management**: integration of people/data/processes/business systems across a product's life cycle (introduction → growth → maturity → decline/retirement); projects/programs intersect this life cycle in multiple patterns (Figure 2-5).
- **Project functions** (2.4): oversight & coordination, solicit & manage feedback, facilitate & support, perform work, apply expertise, provide organizational direction & insight, provide resources — functions, not fixed job titles.
- **Project management team competencies** (Figure 2-7): social responsibilities, power skills, business acumen, ways of working, results.

## Mental Models
- Use "function, not title" when assigning PM responsibilities: a finance manager, scrum master, or product owner can perform the "project manager" function under a different title — judge by the work done, not the label.
- Use situational leadership for the project management team: shift between directive and supportive styles based on project phase criticality and team autonomy needs.
- When sizing organizational structure impact, read PM authority and resource availability together (Table 2-1) — they move together from "low" (functional) to "high" (project-oriented).

## Anti-patterns
- **Judging project success on only one dimension**: calling a project a "failure" purely on cost/schedule overrun (or a "success" purely on hitting budget) ignores whether real value was ever delivered — see the Sydney Opera House vs. Montreal overpass worked example below.
- **Treating OPAs as untouchable**: policies/processes OPAs typically require following organizational policy to change, but project teams are often expected to tailor templates/life cycles/checklists — don't assume everything is fixed, and don't change what requires policy-level sign-off.

## Worked Example

**Project outcome success vs. process success, in contrast:**
- *Sydney Opera House*: initial budget AUS$7M / 4 years → actual AUS$102M / 14 years. By process-efficiency metrics, a clear failure (14x over budget, 3.5x over schedule). But the outcome became a UNESCO World Heritage site drawing 10.9M visitors/year — an outcome success that dwarfs the process failure.
- *Montreal Highway 15 overpass (2016)*: well-managed construction (process success) — but it didn't align with the adjacent Champlain Bridge redevelopment design, so the CA$11M structure had to be demolished a year later — a process success that became a total outcome failure.

Lesson for practitioners: always report both dimensions separately in a project retrospective; a single "green/red" status hides which kind of risk actually materialized.

## Key Takeaways
1. Value delivery depends on closed-loop information flow (Figure 2-3) from senior leadership down to execution and back — check this loop when value isn't materializing despite "on track" status reports.
2. Assess every project on two separate axes: outcome success and process success — don't collapse them into one verdict.
3. Classify constraints as EEF (external/given) vs. OPA (internal/organizational) to know what's tailorable vs. what's a fixed input.
4. Organizational structure type predicts PM authority and resource access — diagnose structure before diagnosing "why doesn't the PM have authority here."
5. PM functions (2.4) are performed by whoever does the work, regardless of job title — map functions to people, not titles, when building a RACI.
