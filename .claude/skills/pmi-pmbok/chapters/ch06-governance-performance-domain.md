# Chapter 6: Governance Performance Domain

## Core Idea
Governance is the holistic, integrative framework of 9 processes that makes decisions to protect and enhance a project's value proposition from initiation through closure — including the authority to cancel it — and it is pervasive throughout the life cycle, not a one-time gate.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Structured vs. Self-Governance (Figure 2-1)**: Structured governance (sponsor + PMO leader + governance board + PM with cross-domain oversight) suits predictive/large projects; Self-governance (distributed PM responsibility across the team) suits adaptive projects, with the key risk being fragmented decision-making — mitigated by clear measurable common objectives + leading indicators + feedback mechanisms.
  - When to use: deciding how much governance structure a project needs — don't default to structured governance for an adaptive team, or self-governance for a regulated predictive megaproject.
- **Three Core Governance Components** (2.1.3): (1) target metrics clearly tied to strategic goals, (2) signaling/alarm mechanisms (leading indicators), (3) feedback mechanisms to assess and improve decision-making — required regardless of structured/self-governed model.
- **Leading vs. Lagging Indicators**: leading = early warning of trends before they cross tolerance (e.g., backlog size, stakeholder unavailability, undefined success criteria — prefer these since they're preventable); lagging = after-the-fact measures (deliverables completed, cost/schedule variance) — easier to measure but reactive.
- **Quality Assurance vs. Quality Control** (distinct efforts, often conflated): QA = using project *processes* effectively so stakeholders trust the eventual result (regulations, compliance, audits); QC = creating *deliverables* that meet defined specs/thresholds (product design, testing, defects).
- **Explicit vs. Tacit Knowledge** (Manage Project Knowledge): explicit = codifiable, formal, easily shared (manuals, databases); tacit = personal, experience-based, hard to articulate/transfer — the core knowledge-management objective is converting tacit → explicit where possible, via mentoring, storytelling, retrospectives, face-to-face interaction (AI interview bots are a newer tool for this).
- **SMART criteria** (2.1.5.3): specific, measurable, achievable, realistic, time-bound — the bar every governance metric should clear.

## Key Concepts
- **Sourcing strategy** (insource/outsource/both): decision driven by culture, specialized-skill need, risk-profile impact, and value proposition — see Reference Table below for make-vs-buy trade-offs.
- **Escalation & Investment control**: two governance components specifically needed in predictive environments — escalation routes blockers to higher authority; investment control applies formal funding stewardship (common in public companies, government agencies, lender-financed construction with staged go/no-go funding decisions).
- **Project governance scenarios** (Table 2-2): initiation, replanning, expansion/contraction, early termination for positive cause (value already achieved), early termination for negative cause (value no longer achievable).
- **Change Control Board (CCB)**: formally reviews/approves/defers/rejects change requests in predictive approaches; in adaptive approaches, change is handled via **backlog management** instead — lower priority = effectively deferred, removal from backlog = effectively rejected, with no formal "approval" step.

## Mental Models
- "Governance is not a one-time activity; it is pervasive throughout the project life cycle" — treat every Focus Area (Initiating through Closing) as having a governance dimension, not just the chartering step.
- Measurement costs time that could go to other work — only measure what's actionable, and SMART-check every metric before adopting it.
- When tailoring governance, ask: is this project's approach predictive (likely heavier/structured governance), adaptive (lighter/self-governance, "just enough" process), or hybrid (mixed, by stream)?

## Anti-patterns — Measurement Pitfalls (2.1.6.7.1)
- **Hawthorne effect**: the act of measuring changes behavior — e.g., measuring only deliverable *volume* pushes teams to produce more low-value deliverables rather than higher-satisfaction ones.
- **Vanity metrics**: numbers that look informative but don't support decisions (e.g., page views vs. new-viewer count).
- **Demoralization**: unachievable targets erode morale even when stretch goals were well-intended.
- **Misusing metrics**: chasing less-important metrics, optimizing short-term at long-term expense, or doing easy-but-out-of-sequence work just to move an indicator.
- **Confirmation bias**: seeing only the data that supports a preexisting view.
- **Correlation vs. causation**: e.g., assuming "behind schedule" *causes* "over budget" when both may share a hidden root cause (poor estimating, weak change/risk management).

## Reference Tables

**Table 2-3 condensed — Insourcing vs. Outsourcing**

| Insource/Make | Outsource/Buy |
|---|---|
| Leverages existing internal expertise | Leverages external expertise the org lacks |
| Cheaper when substantial new innovation is required | Cheaper when goods/services are common/commoditized |
| Tighter integration with strategic/value proposition | Frees internal capacity/capital for core strengths |
| More control and oversight of deliverables | Transfers delivery risk to the supplier |

**Governance Performance Domain — 9 Processes (ITTO pattern, abbreviated)**

| Process | Key Output | Focus Area |
|---|---|---|
| Initiate Project or Phase | Project charter, assumption log | Initiating |
| Integrate and Align Project Plans | Unified project management plan | Planning |
| Plan Sourcing Strategy | Sourcing strategy plan (insource/outsource decisions, source selection criteria) | Planning |
| Manage Project Execution | Deliverables, work performance data, issue log, change requests | Executing |
| Manage Quality Assurance | Quality reports, change requests | Executing |
| Manage Project Knowledge | Lessons learned register | Executing |
| Monitor and Control Project Performance | Work performance reports, change requests | Monitoring & Controlling |
| Assess and Implement Changes | Approved/rejected/deferred change requests | Monitoring & Controlling |
| Close Project or Phase | Final report, accepted deliverables, lessons learned register | Closing |

## Worked Example

**Adaptive project charter (2.1.6.1)**: instead of a full predictive charter, an adaptive project charter can be as light as a vision statement answering: Why is this being undertaken? Who will be engaged? What is it about? Where will it occur? When will it start/end? How will it be carried out? Who is the customer? — "just enough" structure to align stakeholders without locking in detail that will change.

**Change flow (Figure 2-11, predictive)**: Change Request → Integrated Change Control → Impact Analysis across Scope/Finance/Schedule/Resources/Stakeholders/Risk → Decision (Approved / Rejected / Deferred / More Information needed) → if approved, flows as Corrective Action / Preventive Action / Defect Repair / Update back into the same six domains. Use this exact flow as a checklist when designing a change-control process from scratch.

**Table 2-4 — Governance Interactions With Other Performance Domains (summary)**

| Domain | Why the interaction matters |
|---|---|
| Scope | Project value lives in scope, so Governance–Scope is often the single most important domain interaction — formality can vary, but the governance decisions here are pivotal. |
| Schedule | Value proposition is often time-sensitive; well-applied governance speeds delivery, poorly-applied governance slows or derails it. |
| Finance | All projects are investments sensitive to cost — governance quality directly affects cost overrun/underrun risk. |
| Stakeholders | Good governance engages the right stakeholders at the right time so they can advise, approve, or act when needed. |
| Resources | Project completion depends on right resources, right priorities, right timing — governance must coordinate this within the project and across the organization. |
| Risk | Risk mitigation/opportunity capture should align with strategic objectives under clear governance to optimize risk-adjusted return — extra mitigation spend is worth it when it improves safety, compliance, or stakeholder confidence enough. |

**Table 2-5 — Check Results for Governance (sample outcomes → how to check them)**

| Target outcome | How to check it |
|---|---|
| Appropriate oversight by leadership/third parties | Teams know and document agreed-upon processes/protocols/team agreements, aligned with organizational governance artifacts. |
| Governance processes in place, effective, continuously improved | Teams are aware of and act on governance structure, internal/external audits, customer governance requirements; use compliance audits/checklists (predictive) or regular checklist+timeboxing+meetings (adaptive). |
| Effective signaling mechanisms tied to target outcomes | Team selects signaling mechanism(s) that align most closely with maximizing the project's value proposition. |
| Lessons learned/retrospectives managed throughout | Incorporated continuously, not just at project end. |
| Key management kept apprised | Metrics tracked regularly across: status/progress, resource needs, risks + response plans + escalation criteria, assumptions, benefits realized, decision tracking, change control, compliance, communications, issues, funding/financial performance. |

## Key Takeaways
1. Match governance model (structured vs. self-governing) to development approach — predictive/large → structured; adaptive → self-governing with clear objectives and feedback loops to avoid fragmentation.
2. Every governance metric needs all three components — target metric, signaling mechanism, feedback loop — not just a dashboard number.
3. Prefer leading indicators over lagging ones; they're what let you prevent rework instead of just measuring it after the fact.
4. Separate Quality Assurance (process trust) from Quality Control (deliverable spec-conformance) — they use different tools and answer different questions.
5. Actively convert tacit knowledge to explicit knowledge (mentoring, retrospectives, storytelling) — codified-but-context-free knowledge isn't enough on its own.
6. Watch for all six measurement pitfalls before trusting a dashboard — especially Hawthorne effect and correlation/causation confusion.
7. In predictive approaches, changes go through a CCB with explicit approve/reject/defer decisions; in adaptive approaches, the backlog itself *is* the change-control mechanism.
