# Chapter 3: Project Management Principles

## Core Idea
Project management is "principle-based," not prescriptive: six principles describe a mindset (not rules) — Adopt a Holistic View, Focus on Value, Embed Quality Into Processes and Deliverables, Be an Accountable Leader, Integrate Sustainability Within All Project Areas, Build an Empowered Culture — and these mindset dimensions drive the mechanics (performance domains) covered in later chapters.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The Project Management Mindset (Figure 3-1)**: three dimensions, each pairing two principles —
  - **Proactive** = Adopt a Holistic View + Embed Quality Into Processes and Deliverables
  - **Ownership** = Be an Accountable Leader + Build an Empowered Culture
  - **Value-Driven** = Focus on Value + Integrate Sustainability Within All Project Areas
  - When to use: diagnosing a struggling project's root behavioral gap — is it a planning/quality gap (proactive), an accountability/culture gap (ownership), or a value/sustainability gap (value-driven)?
- **The Sustainability Pyramid (Figure 3-7)**: four escalating response levels to a project's negative externalities, most → least desirable: **Avoid** negative outcomes altogether → **Minimize** them → **Restore** impacts → **Compensate/offset**.
  - How: when a project will create a negative externality, work up this pyramid — compensating is the floor, not the goal.
- **Six Principles × Seven Performance Domains cross-map** (Section 3.2): every principle touches every domain, but strength varies — Adopt a Holistic View is strong everywhere; Embed Quality/Focus on Value concentrate in Governance, Scope, Risk, Schedule, Finance, Stakeholders; Be an Accountable Leader concentrates in Governance, Stakeholders, Risk; Integrate Sustainability and Build an Empowered Culture are relevant everywhere.

## Key Concepts
- **Quality dimensions** (Section 3.5): performance, conformity, reliability, resilience, satisfaction, uniformity, efficiency, sustainability, compliance.
- **Leadership vs. authority**: authority is positional control; leadership is inspiring/motivating by example — "any project professional, stakeholder, and team member can demonstrate leadership behaviors."
- **Shared leadership**: leadership can shift to whoever is best positioned in the moment, not fixed to a title.
- **Servant leadership**: respectfulness, humility, availability — leading by supporting the team and removing barriers.
- **Triple bottom line**: balancing people (social equity), planet (environmental stewardship), profit (economic prosperity).
- **Team agreements**: behavioral parameters/working norms set at project start, upheld by individual/team commitment.

## Mental Models
- "Focus on Value" means shifting attention from **deliverable** to **intended outcome** — a delivered output that doesn't enable the outcome (e.g., software without adoption/training) undermines the whole investment.
- Use "shift-left" quality: embed quality reviews as early milestones, not just end-of-phase gates.
- When sustainability goals aren't formally chartered, still raise them in discussions — the principle doesn't require a mandate to apply.

## Anti-patterns
- **Treating "meeting or exceeding value" as license to add scope**: Focus on Value explicitly warns against scope creep/gold-plating even while pursuing value.
- **Confusing authority with leadership**: assuming only the person with positional authority can lead — the standard explicitly separates these and expects shared leadership on high-performing teams.
- **Ignoring sustainability risk until it's a crisis**: prioritizing cost over environmental stewardship or underestimating compliance requirements is called out directly as a sustainability risk pattern.

## Worked Example

**Principle in Action — Focus on Value (3.4.2)**: A company rolling out a new internal technology system. Conventional approach: pick the product with the most features for the price, then customize to every stakeholder request. Value-focused approach instead investigates the organization's actual culture — if it's fast-paced and values simplicity over feature-richness, the team deliberately **reduces** features/customizations to match that culture, increasing adoption and satisfaction versus the feature-maximizing default.

**Principle in Action — Adopt a Holistic View (3.3.2)**: An NGO running a public health education project learns mid-project of a government grant for aligned work. A narrow approach would keep executing the original plan. The holistic approach adapts communications to qualify for the grant, accepting stricter policy constraints in exchange for more resources/reach — a bigger, more holistic win than staying narrowly scoped.

## Key Takeaways
1. Use the three mindset dimensions (proactive/ownership/value-driven) as a quick diagnostic lens for *why* a project is underperforming, before jumping to process fixes.
2. Work up the Sustainability Pyramid — avoid is always preferable to compensate — don't default to offsetting as if it were sufficient.
3. Separate authority (position) from leadership (influence by example); expect and enable shared leadership.
4. Focus on Value = outcome focus, not deliverable focus — ask "does this output actually enable the outcome?" before declaring scope done.
5. Quality has 9 dimensions (not just "meets spec") — check resilience, sustainability, and compliance, not only conformity.
6. All six principles touch all seven performance domains, but with uneven strength — use the Section 3.2 cross-map to know where a given principle has the most leverage.
