# Chapter 10: Stakeholders Performance Domain

## Core Idea
Stakeholders permeate every aspect of a project — they define/prioritize scope, shape planning, set acceptance criteria, and determine whether outcomes are realized — so this domain treats identification, analysis, and engagement as a continuous activity across the whole life cycle, not a one-time kickoff exercise.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Stakeholder spectrum** (supportive / neutral / opponent): every stakeholder-engagement effort should classify people along this axis, internal or external, and revisit the classification routinely (at minimum, each phase transition) since influence/power/interest shift as the project unfolds.
  - When to use: anytime stakeholder engagement planning starts to feel generic — explicitly place each stakeholder/group on this spectrum before designing their engagement approach.
- **Sponsor engagement checklist**: sponsors (often executive-level) are expected to initiate the project/define objectives/business case, approve the charter and its changes, assign/empower the PM, initiate and sit on a steering committee, approve the project management plan and its changes, ensure long-term benefits realization, propose termination if needed, and authorize completion.
  - When to use: diagnosing a "disengaged sponsor" problem — check which of these specific activities the sponsor is actually missing, rather than treating "engagement" as one vague thing.
- **AI/LLM use-decision process** (2.5.3, tailoring): evaluate AI use per-project through an explicit decision process — focus the evaluation on which communication artifacts AI might help produce, and proactively loop in stakeholders like cybersecurity to assess whether the risk of incorporating AI is organizationally acceptable.

## Key Concepts
- **Stakeholder engagement**: activities to identify/analyze stakeholder needs and manage expectations/communications to foster support — "an active project sponsor is a critical success factor" per cited research.
- **Team engagement**: project team members are themselves critical stakeholders, not just labor — their recommendations shape decisions the sponsor/stakeholders ultimately make.
- **Communications management**: closely linked to stakeholder engagement; tailor the *way* of communicating to the stakeholder, not a one-size-fits-all broadcast.
- **Data-driven decision-making**: AI-powered tools (predictive analytics, data visualization) can help PMs prepare decision materials and present recommendations faster/with more confidence — paired with, not replacing, strong leadership/communication skills.
- **Vendor/supplier management**: not core to this domain but closely adjacent — vendors are key stakeholders wherever external resources are used (especially construction-type industries).

## Mental Models
- Stakeholder identification doubles as a risk-management strategy: failing to identify the right stakeholders early, or failing to keep updating that list, directly increases project risk/uncertainty.
- Match communication formality to organizational culture: flat organizations → informal/open channels; hierarchical organizations → more formal approaches. Don't apply one communications style across radically different organizational cultures.
- Treat "stakeholder agreement with project objectives" as measurable (change-request volume, increment feedback), not just a vibe check.

## Anti-patterns
- **Treating identification as a one-time kickoff task**: stakeholders, their influence, and their interests change as the project evolves — the standard explicitly calls for routine review, "at least when the project progresses through phases."
- **Applying a uniform communication method to all stakeholders**: different stakeholders need different channels (email for formal updates vs. instant messaging for quick questions) and different levels of detail based on their role.
- **Adopting AI tools without a risk/ethics check**: the standard explicitly calls for proactive security/ethics review (e.g., looping in cybersecurity) before using AI for stakeholder-facing artifacts.

## Worked Example

**Three tailoring examples (2.5.3.1)**:
1. *Agile*: continuous feedback on features/user stories via daily coordination meetings + dedicated real-time platforms → faster adjustment, higher engagement, faster time to market.
2. *Manufacturing ERP rollout*: formal biweekly email newsletters + monthly town halls, matched to a hierarchical organization needing cross-level alignment.
3. *Medium global project*: multilingual updates + monthly virtual forums, specifically to maintain engagement/feedback across different cultural backgrounds.

Same underlying need (keep stakeholders engaged), three completely different communication designs — chosen by context, not by a default playbook.

## Key Takeaways
1. Re-identify and re-prioritize stakeholders routinely, not just at kickoff — treat it as an ongoing risk-management activity.
2. Use the sponsor-engagement checklist to diagnose "disengaged sponsor" complaints concretely rather than vaguely.
3. Tailor communication channel AND level of detail per stakeholder/role — a complex multi-stakeholder project needs a more sophisticated communications plan than a small one.
4. Before adopting AI tools for stakeholder-facing work, run the explicit risk/ethics check (e.g., with cybersecurity) rather than assuming it's fine.
5. Track stakeholder-domain health with concrete signals (NPS, change-request volume, increment feedback) — not just "stakeholders seem happy."
