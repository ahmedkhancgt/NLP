# Chapter 26: Use cases in edtech industry

## Core Idea
ChatGPT disrupted a $340 billion EdTech sector by resolving the "unstructured data problem" (organizing scattered learning content) and enabling five concrete classroom-level use cases — topic understanding, writing support, language learning, workflow automation, and customized education — while simultaneously raising a genuine academic-integrity risk (easy cheating) that the sector must actively manage.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **5 Classroom-Level EdTech Use Cases**: Understanding the topic (step-by-step Q&A) → Solving writing crisis (topic suggestion, idea generation, essay grading) → Learning a language (AI tutor for translation/pronunciation/grammar/conversation) → Automating workflows (grading, feedback automation freeing teacher time) → Customized education (individualized learning programs per student).
  - When to use: scoping where an EdTech product should integrate ChatGPT first.
  - How: match each use case to its primary beneficiary — students get topic Q&A, writing support, and language practice directly; teachers get workflow automation; the business gets a customization capability to differentiate its product.
- **Unstructured-to-structured data conversion**: ChatGPT's ability to organize messy, unstructured educational content into structured, navigable material.
  - When to use: any EdTech content-management challenge where source material (lecture transcripts, scattered notes, mixed-format resources) needs organizing before it's useful.

## Key Concepts
- **EdTech private investment trajectory**: $16.1B (2020) → $20.8B (2021) → declining to $10.6B (2022), partly attributed to Chinese investor withdrawal.
- **Academic integrity risk**: the chapter explicitly names "it was easy for students to cheat" as a direct consequence of ChatGPT's intellectual power and unlimited access.
- **Technopolicy need**: the chapter's call for government to "think and make robust technopolicy" partnering with private EdTech companies — a direct policy recommendation, not just a technology description.
- **Documented limitation set**: ChatGPT cannot understand complex human emotions/discussion dynamics, humor/sarcasm, or sustain personalized customer support without large data inputs.

## Mental Models
- Use the **"benefit-and-risk-are-the-same-capability" framing**: the chapter is explicit that ChatGPT's intellectual power is simultaneously what makes it pedagogically valuable AND what makes cheating easy — these are not separable features, which is why schools "were required" to incorporate it into teaching procedures rather than simply ban it.
- Treat **"unpack AI tools together" as the recommended teacher stance** — rather than outlawing AI writing tools, the chapter recommends teachers and students jointly examine how they work, building critical media literacy as the mitigation for the cheating risk.

## Key Takeaways
1. EdTech's $340B sector scale meant it "could not ignore" ChatGPT — a framing of inevitability rather than optional adoption.
2. The same capability driving value (instant, accurate, always-available answers) is explicitly the mechanism enabling academic-integrity risk — any EdTech integration plan must address both simultaneously.
3. Teacher-facing workflow automation (grading, feedback) is framed as freeing time for higher-value work (class prep, student engagement), not replacing the teaching role.
4. The sector's funding decline (2021→2022) is cited as context, suggesting AI adoption is happening amid broader EdTech investment headwinds, not during a funding boom.
